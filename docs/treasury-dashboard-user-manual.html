1. What This Dashboard Does

This dashboard forecasts how much money a remittance business will move through a given receiving country, corridor (a sending → receiving country pair), or payout agent over a chosen time horizon — and indicates whether the funding planned for that period would be enough to cover it.

Under the hood it uses Prophet, a statistical forecasting model, trained on the business's actual historical transaction data. Understanding Prophet itself is not required to use the dashboard — only the handful of inputs described below, and how to read the three outputs it returns: a set of headline numbers, two charts, and a day-by-day table.

Every field in the live dashboard has a small "i" icon next to it — hovering or tapping it surfaces the same explanation found in this manual, without leaving the page.

2. A Quick Primer, for Beginners

For readers new to treasury/fintech operations, a few terms are worth grounding before working through the form:

  • Corridor — the path money takes from one country to another, e.g. United Arab Emirates → Nepal. A remittance business typically sends from a small number of countries but pays out into many more.
  • Liquidity / funding — the actual cash a treasury team needs to have on hand, in the right place, to pay out transactions as they land. Too little, and payouts stall or fail; too much sitting idle is wasted capital.
  • Forecasting volume — predicting how much money is likely to flow through a corridor tomorrow, next week, or next quarter, based on how it's behaved historically (including patterns like weekday effects, holidays, and end-of-month payday spikes).
  • Safety stock — a concept borrowed from inventory management: instead of funding for the average day, the funding target is set to a cautious, risk-adjusted estimate, so an unusually busy day does not create a shortfall.

Everything this dashboard does is built from those four ideas: forecast the corridor's volume, add a safety margin, compare it against the funding actually planned, and surface the gap.

6. The "Suggest" Baseline Auto-Fill

Baseline funding level is not calculated automatically — it is a plain input, representing the amount currently planned to be funded each day. The engine's whole purpose is to indicate whether that number is enough. For a data-driven starting point instead of an estimate, the Suggest button next to the field can be used: selecting a method and a trailing window (in days) fills the field from the recent history on record for whatever is currently selected.

MethodWhat it computesBest suited for
AveragePlain mean of the trailing window.General-purpose starting point.
Weighted averageRecency-weighted (EWMA) — recent days count more than older ones in the same window.Corridors whose typical volume has recently shifted.
MedianThe middle value of the window — resistant to one unusually large day.Thin, spiky corridors (rare big transactions on a background of quiet days).

Window (days) defaults to 30 and is adjustable. If less history exists than the window requested, the dashboard uses what is available and reports that plainly — it never silently pads or fabricates data.

8. Advanced Settings, In Depth

Every field below acts on exactly one thing: the single daily time series that Dimension (Section 4) and the core filters produced — whichever receiving country, corridor, or agent was selected. There is no hidden per-corridor breakdown inside a Receiver Country forecast: choosing Receiver Country = Nepal combines every sending corridor into Nepal into one daily total first, and every setting below — seasonality, growth, salary week, holidays — is fit against that combined total, not against each sending corridor separately. Every number on this page is real, pulled directly from the live API for illustration, not invented.

Which public holidays actually apply, by Dimension

Holidays are the one place Dimension changes more than just "which rows get combined" — it also decides which country's public holidays feed into the model at all, since that depends on which country fields the request actually has values for:

Salary week and both flavors of seasonality below are not country-dependent — they're fit purely from the selected series' own daily volume pattern, so they apply identically under all three dimensions. Only public holidays follow the table above.

Safety buffer %

safety_stock = yhat_upper × (1 + safety_buffer_pct) — a plain multiplier on top of the model's own upper estimate for that day. Worked example, using a real day's yhat_upper of $337,016:

Safety buffer %CalculationResulting safety stock
0%$337,016 × 1.00$337,016
10%$337,016 × 1.10$370,717
25%$337,016 × 1.25$421,269

Applies identically regardless of Dimension — it's a pure multiplier on whatever yhat_upper the selected series produced that day, nothing more.

Seasonality — Yearly

The same idea, over a full year instead of a week — captures festival seasons, year-end patterns, and other recurring annual effects the data has enough signal to detect. Visible as the "yearly" panel in the Trend Decomposition chart for whatever is currently selected.

With only about 2.5 years of history behind this data, treat any single sharp yearly peak or trough as a useful signal, not a certainty — there simply aren't many repeats of "the same month" yet to confirm it's a real recurring pattern rather than one unusual year.

Seasonality — Daily

Off by default, and there is no worked example to show for it: volume is already totalled per calendar day before Prophet ever sees the data (Section 5's pipeline diagram), so there is no within-a-day pattern left for this setting to find. Turning it on doesn't break anything — it just gives the model an extra parameter with nothing real to fit. Best left off.

9. How Safety Stock, Baseline, and Shortfall Relate

This is the single most important relationship on the whole dashboard. One picture:

  • Safety stock is calculated by the model — the risk-adjusted amount recommended for that day.
  • Baseline is the amount the dashboard has been told will actually be funded.
  • Daily shortfall = however much Safety stock exceeds Baseline that day (zero if Baseline already covers it).
  • Raising the Baseline field (or lowering Safety buffer %) causes shortfall days to disappear — this is the dashboard's core "what if funding were increased" experiment.

13. Evaluating Forecast Accuracy (Backtest)

Every result described so far assumes the forecast can be trusted. The Evaluate forecast accuracy panel — its own collapsible section, below the main results — tests that assumption directly instead of assuming it: it hides a recent stretch of real history, re-trains the model without that stretch, forecasts exactly that hidden period, and compares the forecast against what actually happened. This is called a backtest, and it works independently of the main form — it can be run before or after "Run forecast," using whichever Dimension, filters, and Advanced Settings are currently selected above.

This is a second model fit, separate from the main forecast — it takes a few extra seconds to run and does not happen automatically. Click Run backtest to trigger it whenever the current selection is worth double-checking.

Days to hold out

The one input this panel adds beyond what's already selected above:

FieldWhat it meansRangeDefault
Days to hold outHow many of the most recent days to treat as the test window — hidden from the model while it re-trains, then forecast and compared against what really happened on those days. 7 – 18030
Matching this to Horizon (Section 5) gives the most directly relevant answer — "how accurate has this model been over a period the same length as what I'm about to forecast?" A corridor with very little history may not support a large value here; the panel reports a clear error rather than guessing if there isn't enough history to both train on and hold out.

What happens once Run backtest is clicked:

The five results

ResultAnswers
MAPEOn average, how far off was the forecast, as a percentage? Lower is better. Days with zero actual volume are left out of this one specifically — a percentage error is undefined when the true value is zero, which a quiet corridor genuinely has some days.
RMSEThe same question in dollars rather than percent, and penalizes a few very large misses more than many small ones. Covers every day, including the zero-volume ones MAPE leaves out.
Interval coverageOut of the held-out days, what percentage actually landed inside the model's own Lower–Upper range? Compare this against Interval width (e.g. 80%) — well below that target means the model's own uncertainty band is too narrow to take at face value.
Safety stock coverageOut of the held-out days, what percentage would Safety stock (Upper plus any Safety buffer %) actually have covered? The more directly useful of the two coverage numbers, since it answers "would the real funding recommendation have been enough," not just "was the raw forecast's own uncertainty honest."
vs. naive baselineA sanity check: a flat prediction (just the recent trailing average, held constant across the held-out window — the same number the Suggest → Average method would produce) is scored the same way. If Prophet doesn't beat "just fund the recent average every day" on this selection, the model isn't adding value here.

Reading the backtest chart

Below the five results, a chart plots the held-out window itself — what the model predicted against what really happened, day by day:

A real, verified example

Receiver Country = Nepal, Days to hold out = 14, run directly against the live dataset:

ResultValue
Trained on929 days · tested on 2026-07-18 to 2026-07-31
MAPE279.0% (naive baseline: 274.9%)
RMSE$132,361 (naive baseline: $120,139)
Interval coverage35.7% (target 80%)

A real, useful finding rather than a demonstration of failure: Interval coverage falling well short of its 80% target means this corridor's raw uncertainty band is too narrow to trust as stated — but Safety stock coverage sitting at 100% shows the funding recommendation itself (Upper plus whatever Safety buffer % is configured) was still enough on every one of these 14 days. And Prophet trailing the naive baseline on both MAPE and RMSE here is exactly the kind of honest signal this panel exists to surface — see the next scenario in Section 14 for what to do when that happens.

One held-out window is a sample, not a guarantee — most of all on a thin or spiky corridor with little history (the same idea as the Safety stock ratio caveat in Section 14). A single window can look better or worse than the model's true accuracy by chance. Treat a low coverage number as a signal worth a second look, not a final verdict on the model.

14. Common Scenarios & What To Do

"Safety stock ratio" looks very high (well above 2x)

This usually means the corridor is thin or spiky — many quiet days punctuated by the occasional large transaction — not a modeling error. The Median baseline-suggestion method is often a better fit than Average in this situation, since it resists being pulled up by rare large days.

"No transactions match the given filters"

The combination selected has no data at all under the statuses currently counted — most often a corridor pair that does not exist in the dataset, or a status filter (see Advanced Settings) too narrow for a thin agent. Confirming the corridor/agent selected in the dropdowns — which only ever list real values from the data — resolves most such cases.

The forecast horizon doesn't start "today"

Expected — see the callout in Section 5. It starts the day after the selected filter's own most recent transaction.

Adjusting the safety margin beyond the model's own recommendation

Safety buffer % in Advanced Settings stacks an extra percentage on top of the model's own risk-adjusted number, without changing the forecast itself.

The backtest shows low coverage, or Prophet losing to the naive baseline

Neither means the dashboard is broken. Low Interval coverage means the raw uncertainty band is narrower than it should be for this selection — Safety stock coverage is usually the more reassuring number to check next, since Safety buffer % (Advanced Settings) widens the actual funding recommendation beyond the raw range. Losing to the naive baseline on a thin or spiky corridor is common and worth knowing, not alarming — it means a simple trailing average happened to do as well or better over that particular held-out window. Trying a different Days to hold out value, or checking whether the corridor has enough history (Section 8's yearly-seasonality caution applies here too), are the first things worth trying before assuming something is wrong.
